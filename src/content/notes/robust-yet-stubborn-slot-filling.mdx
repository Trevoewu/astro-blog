## Abstract

Zero-shot cross-domain slot filling faces a sharp asymmetry between two model classes. Frontier black-box large language models (LLMs) generalize broadly to unseen slots, yet they make frequent boundary errors and cannot be fine-tuned locally. Conversely, compact open-weight models can be fine-tuned to enforce strict span boundaries and output formatting, yet they lack broad coverage on unseen slots. **Advisor-guided slot filling** bridges this gap by pairing a frozen black-box LLM *advisor* with a source-trained open-weight *extractor*. Yet even when the advisor supplies an exact gold span on an unseen slot, the fine-tuned extractor frequently overrides it.

We study why source fine-tuning turns a controllable base model into a **robust yet stubborn** extractor across SNIPS and ATIS, and establish three findings. First, stubbornness sets in within the first 200 training steps—long before noise rejection finishes improving—and cannot be separated from noise robustness by early stopping, LoRA weight scaling, or marking unseen slots. Second, robustness and stubbornness stem from the exact same source-domain priors: in ATIS, the extractor moves valid role-qualified spans into generic flat slots; in SNIPS, it repeatedly rejects common words—such as `"my"` for `playlist_owner`—because source training saw them hundreds of times with zero gold labels. Third, these two mechanisms clarify what inference-time mitigations can and cannot achieve without changing model weights: a label-free slot-name gate mitigates wrong-slot copying on ATIS, whereas recurring word omissions reflect an identifiability gap that no zero-label judge resolves. Confirming one to three examples per recurring word pair offers a cheap, low-regression patch—trading the higher F1 ceiling of few-shot fine-tuning for minimal labeling effort—while leaving the underlying stubbornness itself an open training problem.

## 1. Introduction

Zero-shot cross-domain slot filling extracts target-domain slot values from user utterances without labeled target training data ([Liu et al., 2020](#ref-liu-2020); [Yu et al., 2021](#ref-yu-2021)). In practice, practitioners face a sharp trade-off between two classes of models. **Frontier black-box large language models (LLMs)** generalize broadly to unseen target slots, yet they cannot be fine-tuned locally and often produce loose span boundaries when prompted directly. Conversely, **compact open-weight LLMs** can be fine-tuned cheaply on source domains to enforce strict span boundaries and output formatting, yet they lack broad coverage on unfamiliar target slots.

**Advisor-guided extraction** combines both strengths through a simple division of labor:

- The **advisor** is a frozen, black-box frontier LLM that reads the utterance and target schema to propose candidate spans for each slot. Because the advisor is never modified, it can be swapped freely across model providers.
- The **extractor** is a compact, open-weight LLM fine-tuned on source-domain data to make the final prediction from the utterance, schema, and the advisor's suggestions.

Across cross-domain benchmarks and advisor models, this hybrid setup consistently outperforms running either model alone.

Yet this division of labor creates an unexpected puzzle. Given the same advisor suggestions, an untuned base model is **controllable but gullible**: it accepts nearly all valid advice, but it also copies most of the advisor's mistakes. Fine-tuning on source domains flips the extractor into a **robust yet stubborn** state. It learns to filter out noisy advice, but it also begins overriding the advisor—even when the advisor supplies the exact gold span on an unseen slot.

This stubbornness shows up in two concrete ways. On SNIPS, the tuned extractor repeatedly drops words where datasets differ on how to map everyday speech into copied spans. In *"add this track to my global funk"*, for instance, `"my"` simply means the user's own playlist rather than a named owner—but because slot filling requires copying verbatim substrings rather than rewriting to `user`, the target domain tags `"my"` as `playlist_owner`. Other SNIPS domains treat `"my"` as an ordinary pronoun and leave it unlabeled, teaching the extractor to ignore it. At test time, the untuned base model accepts the advisor's suggestion `playlist_owner="my"` every time, whereas the source-tuned extractor rejects it every time. On ATIS, a second pattern appears: instead of dropping a valid advisor span, the tuned extractor moves it from a specific role slot (such as `fromloc.city_name`) into a generic slot (such as `city_name`).

Why does source fine-tuning make an extractor **robust yet stubborn**, and how far can inference-time mitigations go without retraining? To track this shift, Section 2 measures two behaviors (adapting [Li et al., 2022](#ref-li-2022); [Goyal et al., 2025](#ref-goyal-2025)): **conflict controllability** ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$), how often the extractor accepts valid advice when its own prediction is wrong, and **strict noise robustness** ($\mathcal{R}$), how often it keeps a correct prediction when the advice is wrong. Our study answers this question in three steps:

- **Stubbornness sets in early and cannot be tuned away by strength or slot markers (Section 3).** Willingness to follow conflicting advice collapses within the first 200 training steps, long before noise rejection finishes improving. Scaling down LoRA adapter weights at inference time offers no sweet spot where both behaviors stay strong, and the collapse occurs equally on familiar and unfamiliar slots—so marking unseen slots or selectively routing uncertain ones fails to restore deference.
- **Robustness and stubbornness come from the same source-domain priors (Section 4).** Overrides are not random failures on rare slots; they concentrate on two learned source habits. On ATIS, flat-schema training pushes spans out of role-qualified slots into generic base slots; on SNIPS, words like `"my"`, `"this"`, and `"here"` appeared hundreds of times in source training with *zero* gold labels, teaching the extractor to reject them on sight. Swapping `"my"` for an ordinary name in the same prompt immediately restores compliance—confirming a targeted word-refusal prior rather than broken advice-following.
- **Inference-time mitigations alleviate both symptoms but do not cure stubbornness (Section 5).** Because wrong-slot copying moves spans between slots with the same base name (such as `fromloc.city_name` and `city_name`), a label-free schema gate mitigates ATIS errors with zero side effects on SNIPS. By contrast, no zero-label method—not even stronger LLM judges—can separate valid target word conventions from systematic advisor mistakes. Confirming one to three examples per recurring conflict pair patches those recurring disputes cheaply with near-zero regressions, though it yields a smaller F1 gain than full few-shot fine-tuning and leaves the extractor's underlying stubbornness untouched.

## 2. Experimental Setup

### 2.1 Benchmarks and Models

We pair a compact open-weight **extractor**—`Qwen3-8B` ([Yang et al., 2025](#ref-yang-2025)) fine-tuned on labeled source domains via LoRA—with a frozen frontier LLM **advisor** that proposes candidate spans for each slot. Running the extractor without advisor suggestions gives its `Solo` baseline.

We evaluate zero-shot transfer on two English benchmarks:

- **SNIPS** ([Coucke et al., 2018](#ref-coucke-2018)): Seven everyday voice-assistant domains evaluated leave-one-domain-out ([Liu et al., 2020](#ref-liu-2020)), training on six domains and testing on the held-out seventh. Because flat slot names recur across domains, each target domain mixes **source-seen slots** with **unseen target slots**.
- **SNIPS-to-ATIS** ([Hemphill et al., 1990](#ref-hemphill-1990)): Extractors trained on SNIPS are evaluated zero-shot on ATIS flight-booking queries. ATIS uses hierarchical, role-qualified slot names (such as `fromloc.city_name`) that share no names with SNIPS, placing every target slot in the unseen set.

### 2.2 Measuring Controllability vs. Noise Robustness

For each slot, comparing the extractor's `Solo` prediction against the advisor's suggestion divides every advised prediction into four cases (Table 1).

|                  | **Advice correct**                                       | **Advice wrong**                 |
| :--------------- | :------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| **Solo correct** | Agreement preservation                                   | Noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$) |
| **Solo wrong**   | Conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$) | Both-wrong recovery              |

*Table 1: Four-cell breakdown of advisor–extractor interaction on a single slot. Off-diagonal conflict cells isolate conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$) and strict noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$).*

Adapting [Li et al. (2022)](#ref-li-2022), we focus on the two off-diagonal conflict cells:

- **Conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$):** When `Solo` is wrong and the advisor supplies the exact gold span, how often does the extractor adopt the advisor's answer?
- **Noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$):** When `Solo` is right and the advisor supplies a wrong suggestion, how often does the extractor hold its ground?

On the two diagonal cells, the untuned base model and the fine-tuned extractor behave almost identically: when both agree on a correct answer, both keep it over 99% of the time; when both are wrong, neither recovers often ($<7\%$). The behavioral shift from source fine-tuning happens almost entirely when the extractor and advisor disagree.

## 3. The Stubbornness Paradox and What Does Not Explain It

Why does source fine-tuning flip a controllable base model into a **robust yet stubborn** extractor? We first test—and rule out—two natural explanations:

- **Is fine-tuning simply pushed too far?** Can we find a middle ground—either by stopping training early or by scaling down the LoRA weight at inference time—where the model rejects noise without overriding valid advice?
- **Does unfamiliarity with new slot names keep the model from judging advice?** When a target slot is unseen, does the extractor lack the knowledge to tell whether the advisor's suggestion is valid—and would explicitly marking unseen slots teach it to defer?

### 3.1 No "Sweet Spot" in Fine-Tuning Strength

If stubbornness came from pushing fine-tuning too far, moderating its strength—either by stopping training early or by dialing back the LoRA adapter weight at inference time—should catch a middle ground. Testing both axes shows that no such sweet spot exists: stubbornness sets in well before noise rejection matures.

*Figure 1: Conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$) and noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$) across fine-tuning steps on SNIPS. Willingness to accept conflicting advice collapses early in training on both seen and unseen slots, long before noise rejection plateaus.*

**Stopping early in training.** Figure 1 tracks conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$) and strict noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$) across training checkpoints on SNIPS. Between steps 100 and 200—at the very start of fine-tuning—$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$ plunges from 65.3% to 41.0% and stays flat for the rest of training, while noise robustness $\mathcal{R}$ is still climbing toward its plateau. Crucially, this early drop holds even on a fixed set of conflict instances across all checkpoints (confirming it is not an artifact of `Solo` accuracy improving over time) and happens in lockstep on both **source-seen slots** and **unseen target slots**. Rather than slowly overfitting late in training, supervised fine-tuning triggers an immediate shift toward trusting internal parameters over external advice, mirroring context-parametric inversion in instruction tuning ([Goyal et al., 2025](#ref-goyal-2025)).

<AdapterDial />

*Figure 2: Scaling the LoRA adapter weight $\lambda$ from $0$ (untuned base) to $1$ (full adapter) at inference time. Across both benchmarks, conflict controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}}$) drops sharply as $\lambda$ increases, leaving no scale where both controllability and noise robustness ($\mathcal{R}$) remain high.*

**Scaling down adapter weights at inference time.** A natural alternative is to train the extractor fully, then dial back its LoRA adapter weight $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ at inference time to blend the untuned base model ($\lambda = 0$) with the fine-tuned extractor ($\lambda = 1$). Figure 2 shows that this runs into the exact same trade-off. On SNIPS, keeping the adapter weak ($\lambda \le 0.25$) preserves high controllability ($\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{conf}} \ge 85.2\%$), but leaves noise robustness too low to filter out bad advice ($\mathcal{R} \le 30.2\%$). Pushing $\lambda$ from $0.25$ to $1.0$ raises noise robustness to $89.0\%$, but steadily erodes conflict controllability down to $49.4\%$. The same tension holds on ATIS: as $\lambda$ increases from $0.5$ to $1.0$, noise robustness climbs from $27.8\%$ to $69.6\%$, while controllability drops from $91.7\%$ to $57.4\%$—pulling advised F1 down from $71.44$ to $66.68$. Neither stopping training early nor weakening the adapter at inference time offers a sweet spot that separates noise rejection from stubbornness.

### 3.2 Unfamiliar Slot Names Are Not the Cause

Another natural hypothesis is that on unseen target slots, the extractor does not know the new slot names well enough to judge the advisor's suggestion, and would defer to the advisor if explicitly told which slots are unfamiliar. Because a slot marker never seen during training would be meaningless at test time, we tested this by dropping 10 source-domain slots from nearly all training schemas—retaining only a small number of exposures tagged as `[new]` while marking all fully trained slots as `[familiar]`—and then applying the `[new]` marker to truly unseen target slots at test time.

If unfamiliarity with slot names drove stubbornness, the extractor should struggle on those exposure-starved slots when run alone (`Solo`) and defer to the advisor when given the `[new]` marker on unseen target slots. Neither holds:
- **Slot names alone are already easy to read:** By step 50—after seeing an average of just ~2 positive examples per held-out slot—`Solo` accuracy on those 10 slots already reaches **87%** (and exceeds **95%** by convergence), showing that the pretrained model understands standard slot names almost immediately once it learns the output format.
- **Explicit unfamiliarity markers do not cure stubbornness:** On truly unseen target slots at test time, adding the `[new]` marker barely moves conflict controllability ($+2.1$ points over an untagged control), fails to fix `playlist_owner="my"` refusal (**2.5%** compliance), and lowers noise robustness on new slots by **17 points**.

The extractor does not override the advisor because new slot names are opaque; it overrides the advisor when its learned source priors actively oppose the suggested span. Selective routing by token confidence runs into the exact same wall: when source priors oppose a target convention (such as rejecting `"my"` on `AddToPlaylist`), the extractor is *highly confident* in its `NONE` prediction, so routing only cuts query cost without curing stubbornness.

## 4. Why Robustness and Stubbornness Share the Same Root

Why can neither training strength nor slot markers separate noise robustness from stubbornness? Inspecting what the fine-tuned extractor actually overrides shows that **both behaviors come from the exact same source-domain priors**. Learning which words stay unlabeled and how source schemas are structured is precisely what makes the extractor robust against noisy advice—and those same priors cause it to override valid target suggestions whenever target conventions diverge.

### 4.1 Two Conflict Patterns: Slot Reassignment vs. Clustered Word Omission

When the source-tuned extractor overrides valid advice across ATIS and SNIPS, its errors are not random noise on rare utterances. Instead, they split into two systematic patterns:

1. **Schema-bias slot reassignment (ATIS):** On ATIS, where target slots form hierarchies that qualify generic concepts by role (such as `fromloc.city_name` and `toloc.city_name` alongside bare `city_name`), the tuned extractor rarely deletes the advisor's span outright. Instead, it moves the span to a less specific slot: **91.6% of all entities lost** when overriding the advisor immediately reappear under another slot name, almost always shifting from a role-qualified slot into its generic base slot.
2. **Clustered word omission (SNIPS and residual ATIS):** On SNIPS, where slot names are flat, the extractor overrides valid advisor spans by outputting `NONE`. These omissions stem from the span-copy constraint of extractive slot filling: because the model must copy verbatim words from the utterance rather than rewriting them (such as normalizing `"my"` to `user` or `"here"` to `user_location`), annotators must decide whether pronouns and deictic words—like `"my"` for `playlist_owner`, `"this"` for `object_select`, or `"here"` for `current_location`—count as slot spans or stay unlabeled. Instead of scattering randomly across utterances, over two-thirds of all SNIPS conflicts repeat on a few dozen recurring `(slot, span)` pairs. The same concentration holds on ATIS: once wrong-slot copies are set aside, almost all remaining conflicts come from just five recurring word pairs, such as tagging `"after"` under `depart_time.time_relative` or the generic word `"flight"` under `flight`.

### 4.2 Source Training Actively Teaches the Opposite Rule

| Target Slot        | Span     | Source Occurrences (Gold Labels) | Target Validation Gold Rate |
| :----------------- | :------- | -------------------------------: | --------------------------: |
| `playlist_owner`   | `"my"`   |                     375 (0 gold) |                       99.2% |
| `object_select`    | `"this"` |                     624 (0 gold) |                       97.2% |
| `current_location` | `"here"` |                      65 (0 gold) |                       95.2% |

*Table 2: Source-training frequency versus target-domain gold rate for representative SNIPS conflict pairs. The extractor saw these exact words hundreds of times in source domains with zero gold labels, learning a strong negative prior that directly contradicts the target domain's annotation convention.*

Why does the fine-tuned extractor refuse these specific words so stubbornly? Counting how often they appeared in the six SNIPS source-training domains shows that the extractor is not unfamiliar with them—it was repeatedly trained to leave them unlabeled (Table 2).

When training the extractor for the held-out `AddToPlaylist` domain, the word `"my"` appears **375 times** across source training utterances with **0** gold slot labels. Source fine-tuning therefore teaches a strict negative rule: whenever `"my"` appears in an utterance, leave it out. At test time on `AddToPlaylist`, where `"my"` is the gold span for `playlist_owner` **99.2%** of the time, this learned rule collides head-on with the target convention: the untuned base model accepts `playlist_owner="my"` 100% of the time, whereas the source-tuned extractor accepts it **0%** of the time. The exact same contradiction explains why the extractor refuses `"this"` for `object_select` and `"here"` for `current_location`.

Crucially, this refusal targets the penalized words themselves rather than breaking the extractor's ability to handle `playlist_owner` or follow advice. If we take the exact same conflict prompts—where the source-tuned extractor rejects `"my"` **100%** of the time—and simply replace `"my"` with an ordinary name (`"daphne s"`), the tuned extractor immediately resumes following the advisor, raising acceptance from 0% back to **56%–96%** across seeds. Source fine-tuning thus leaves the model's advice-following mechanism intact and only blocks the specific words it was trained to leave unlabeled.

## 5. Implications: What Inference-Time Mitigations Can and Cannot Do

Because weakening or retraining the fine-tuned extractor risks eroding its noise robustness, a natural practical question is how much of its stubbornness can be alleviated at inference time without changing model weights. Our diagnosis in Section 4 yields three concrete conclusions (full experimental tables are provided in the [Appendix](#appendix-inference-time-mitigation-experiments)):

- **Hierarchical wrong-slot copying can be mitigated without target labels ([Appendix A.1](#a1-zero-label-schema-gate)).** On ATIS, role-qualified slots and generic fallback slots share the same base name after the final dot (such as `fromloc.city_name` and `city_name`). A deterministic **Same-Family Span-Binding Gate** that routes hijacked spans back to the matching advisor slot lifts ATIS F1 by **+11.55 points** (surpassing raw advisor suggestions), while staying completely dormant (**0.00 $\Delta$F1**) on flat schemas like SNIPS.
- **Word-level conventions cannot be resolved without target labels ([Appendix A.2](#a2-zero-label-judges-on-word-conventions)).** Recurring SNIPS conflicts mix valid target conventions (`playlist_owner="my"`, `object_select="this"`) with systematic advisor mistakes (`condition_description="weather"`, `object_type="movies"`). Given only terse slot descriptions, every zero-label method fails in one of two opposite directions: blanket overrides and external LLM judges over-accept plausible words (yielding noisy fix-to-harm ratios of `0.69:1`–`2.27:1`), whereas the untuned base model and source-domain lookup over-reject valid conventions. Word-level conventions represent an **identifiability gap, not a reasoning gap**.
- **Minimal human confirmation is a lightweight patch, not a cure ([Appendix A.3](#a3-pair-confirmation-vs-few-shot-supervision)).** Because each recurring `(slot, span)` pair follows a consistent target rule, confirming just 1–3 example utterances per pair via binary `yes`/`no` questions recovers **+2.47 to +2.88 F1** on SNIPS (and adds **+2.46 F1** on top of the ATIS gate) with near-zero regressions (only 20 broken predictions). Compared with 80-utterance few-shot fine-tuning, this reflects a clear trade-off rather than a single winner: few-shot fine-tuning achieves more than double the F1 gain (`+6.36` vs. `+2.88`) by learning patterns beyond recurring conflicts, at the cost of much heavier span annotation and 2,753 broken predictions.

## 6. Conclusion

Source fine-tuning turns an open-weight slot-filling extractor from **controllable yet gullible** into **robust yet stubborn**—and these two properties are bound together from the very start of training. The same source-domain priors that teach the extractor to filter noisy advisor suggestions (flat schema structure and negative priors on unlabeled pronouns) also cause it to override valid target advice. Inference-time workarounds—a schema-based gate for hierarchical wrong-slot copying and 1–3 binary confirmations per recurring word pair—can alleviate these two dominant symptoms without sacrificing noise robustness, but they are external patches rather than a cure. Truly decoupling noise robustness from parametric stubbornness during training remains an open problem.

## 7. Limitations

- **Terse schema descriptions.** Both SNIPS and ATIS provide short, 1–6 word slot descriptions (such as `"owner"` for `playlist_owner` or `"weather"` for `condition_description`). Writing detailed, guideline-style definitions ahead of deployment would give zero-label LLM judges ([Appendix A.2](#a2-zero-label-judges-on-word-conventions)) much clearer boundary rules, though it would not by itself fix the fine-tuned extractor's stubbornness: in both failure modes, the advisor already places the right span in the right slot—and even when the description explicitly states `"city name of from location"` (`fromloc.city_name`) or `"this current"` (`object_select`), the source-tuned extractor still overrides it using its learned priors.
- **Benchmark and task scope.** Our experiments focus on two English single-turn slot-filling benchmarks—leave-one-domain-out transfer on SNIPS and cross-dataset transfer to ATIS. Extending this analysis to multilingual schemas and multi-turn dialogue state tracking benchmarks (such as MultiWOZ) remains an important direction for future work.
- **Single extractor architecture.** Although we evaluate four frozen advisors and external LLM judges spanning multiple model families, our fine-tuned extractor is always `Qwen3-8B`. Investigating how extractor scale or alternative fine-tuning objectives (such as context-aware regularization) shape the early controllability–robustness trade-off is left to future work.

## 8. Related Work

- **Zero-shot cross-domain slot filling.** Early cross-domain slot filling transfers to unseen target domains by conditioning sequence taggers on slot descriptions ([Bapna et al., 2017](#ref-bapna-2017); [Lee & Jha, 2019](#ref-lee-2019)) or example values ([Shah et al., 2019](#ref-shah-2019)), with context-aware representations ([Siddique et al., 2021](#ref-siddique-2021)), decoupled span detection and typing ([Liu et al., 2020](#ref-liu-2020)), and contrastive slot sharpening ([He et al., 2020](#ref-he-2020); [Zhang & Zhang, 2023](#ref-zhang-2023)) improving generalization. Subsequent work casts slot filling as question answering or reading comprehension ([Du et al., 2021](#ref-du-2021); [Yu et al., 2021](#ref-yu-2021); [Liu et al., 2022](#ref-liu-2022); [Liu et al., 2025](#ref-liu-2025)), generative prompting ([Yan et al., 2022](#ref-yan-2022); [Li et al., 2023](#ref-li-2023)), or retrieval-augmented demonstration matching ([Song et al., 2025](#ref-song-2025)). Because prompted LLMs alone struggle with strict span boundaries ([Pan et al., 2023](#ref-pan-2023)), staged prompting ([Zhu et al., 2024](#ref-zhu-2024)), instruction tuning ([Mirza et al., 2024](#ref-mirza-2024)), teacher distillation ([Rana et al., 2025](#ref-rana-2025)), and synthetic data generation ([Huang et al., 2025](#ref-huang-2025)) have been proposed to narrow this gap, building on schema-guided dialogue interfaces ([Rastogi et al., 2020](#ref-rastogi-2020); [Lin et al., 2021](#ref-lin-2021); [Zhao et al., 2022](#ref-zhao-2022)).
- **Small–large model collaboration.** Recent work pairs compact extractors with larger LLMs in complementary roles. In GRSF ([Li et al., 2025](#ref-li-2025-grsf)), an LLM generates slot knowledge filtered by a trained evaluator for a small tagger; in SynAug ([Li et al., 2025](#ref-li-2025-synaug)) and SuperICL ([Xu et al., 2024](#ref-xu-2024)), a small model's predictions are injected into an LLM prompt that makes the final call. Other hybrids route uncertain predictions from a small model to an LLM ([Chen & Wang, 2025](#ref-chen-2025)), learn question-generation strategies for zero-shot QA ([Tavares et al., 2023](#ref-tavares-2023)), condition on an LLM guidance prefix ([Yao et al., 2024](#ref-yao-2024)), or use reinforcement learning to decide when a 7B NER model should invoke external search ([Chen et al., 2026](#ref-chen-2026)). Closest in terminology, [Asawa et al. (2026)](#ref-asawa-2026) train a small RL advisor to steer a frozen black-box LLM with free-form advice. Our setting reverses this asymmetry: a frozen LLM advisor proposes candidate slot spans to a source-trained 8B extractor, isolating how supervised fine-tuning governs the extractor's willingness to follow or override external advice.
- **Controllability, noise robustness, and context-parametric inversion.** Balancing external context against internal parameters is a central tension in conditional generation. [Li et al. (2022)](#ref-li-2022) formalize *controllability* (following conflicting context) versus *robustness* (ignoring irrelevant context) and propose Knowledge-Aware Fine-Tuning (KAFT) to preserve both. Similarly, RAFT ([Zhang et al., 2024](#ref-zhang-2024)) and [Yoran et al. (2024)](#ref-yoran-2024) train generators on mixtures of relevant and distractor passages so they ignore noisy context. However, [Goyal et al. (2025)](#ref-goyal-2025) show that instruction fine-tuning triggers *context-parametric inversion*: reliance on external context rises early in training and then collapses as routine examples dominate gradient updates. We observe an analogous shift in advisor-guided slot filling: source fine-tuning makes the extractor robust to noisy advice while collapsing controllability on conflicting spans, producing both word-level convention refusals and wrong-slot copying.
- **Guideline refinement and interactive weak supervision.** Several recent frameworks address schema ambiguity through annotation guidelines or weak-supervision feedback. GoLLIE ([Sainz et al., 2024](#ref-sainz-2024)) trains extraction models to follow detailed Python-formatted guidelines, assuming those target guidelines are already written and available in the prompt. DiZiNER ([Kim & Yoon, 2026](#ref-kim-2026)) simulates pilot annotation across multiple LLMs and refines zero-shot NER instructions from inter-model disagreements without target labels. Interactive weak supervision ([Boecking et al., 2021](#ref-boecking-2021)) queries annotators on candidate heuristic rules rather than individual examples. Our setting differs from all three: standard slot-filling schemas supply only terse slot descriptions without boundary rules, inter-model disagreement cannot reveal which convention a target dataset actually follows ([Section 5](#5-implications-what-inference-time-mitigations-can-and-cannot-do)), and confirming 1–3 examples per recurring conflict pair offers a lighter, lower-regression trade-off than utterance-level few-shot fine-tuning ([Appendix A.3](#a3-pair-confirmation-vs-few-shot-supervision)).
