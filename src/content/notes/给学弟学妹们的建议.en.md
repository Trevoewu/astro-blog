It's hard to believe how quickly college has come to an end. This piece was originally supposed to be advice for younger students, as requested by the school—but for someone as unremarkable and unsuccessful as me, I don't really feel qualified to be giving advice.

#### Looking back at freshman year, what would I tell myself now?

> "I have never let my schooling interfere with my education." — Mark Twain

At first, I wanted to tell myself: learn how to get around the firewall ("climb the wall") and learn from the vast internet, master a programming language, and stick with learning English. Learn to use VS Code, Vim, Markdown, Docker, Git, and the terminal. Learn how to rent a server, buy a domain, set up your own repositories on GitHub, and build your own blog. Study courses like Calculus, Linear Algebra, Probability Theory, Discrete Mathematics, Computer Networks, Data Structures, Operating Systems, Compilers, and Computer Organization seriously.

I used to blame myself all the time—for not picking up these skills earlier, and for not realizing how important these courses were. As a result, I spent my days rushing around in a flood of pointless busywork with very little to show for it. Last month, [jyy](https://space.bilibili.com/202224425) from Nanjing University uploaded a [recorded lecture](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1MV411F738) on Bilibili meant for competitive programmers ("IOers") in Jiangsu. In the video, jyy explained why competitive programming environments have all moved to Linux, and taught the students basic Linux commands, practical tips, and how to configure a programming environment. Watching that video, I couldn't help feeling envious—envious that they got to encounter Linux so early, and envious that they had a teacher like jyy telling them not to rely solely on Windows' graphical interface, and showing them that all those powerful features in an IDE are actually built from small, fundamental tools like the GNU utilities.

Back in freshman year, I was still clumsily using an ancient IDE like Code::Blocks, scavenging through garbage dumps like CSDN and Baidu, scratching my head over a nested `for` loop, and completely clueless when faced with bubble sort. Even so, I won't be too hard on myself, nor will I demand that my past self should have done things differently. Because I know that back then, I had just graduated from high school, still stuck in the quagmire of exam-oriented education, knowing nothing about how the world worked. Nobody told me what I ought to be doing or which tools I should learn. College, to me, was uncharted territory—an admission ticket earned through three years of ascetic, monk-like high school life. Stepping into this unfamiliar world, I was full of hope and longing for everything good. I joined clubs and the college debate team, actively made friends, and fantasized about having a fresh "college debut." Holding onto the mindset of a "good student" from high school, I showed up for morning exercises and never skipped a single class, just spinning in place without ever stopping.

I don't blame myself, because I know that under those circumstances, I was already trying hard enough. I wouldn't ask that version of me to have mastered all those tools. If there is anything I truly wish I had known back then, it's this: **don't give up on learning English, buy a Mac, and learn to skip class**.

#### Giving up my high school habit of memorizing English vocabulary during freshman and sophomore year is my one real regret so far

> "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world." — Ludwig Wittgenstein

Back then, I had no idea how valuable English would be for opening a window onto the world and learning from the rich, high-quality resources available online. It's also a sorrow—one that comes with being a native Chinese speaker. There are simply far too few high-quality Chinese resources on the internet. It's not that good content doesn't exist on the Simplified Chinese web, but rather that it's locked away like private Moments feeds—impossible to find through search engines—leaving Baidu flooded with ads and spam. A year or two ago, when I had just learned how to use GitHub, I saw a programmer write in their project's README: *"I hope everyone will write READMEs in Chinese so we can build a healthy Chinese programming ecosystem together."* It's genuinely sad—Chinese makes up such a tiny fraction of the internet. According to one statistical report, Chinese-language content accounts for just over 1% of the web. Nobody expects it to match English's dominant position, of course, but with a population of 1.3 billion and a billion internet users, China's output of Chinese content on the internet is barely 1%—trailing not just Japanese, Russian, and French, but even surpassed by Vietnamese. Now that we've entered the era of AIGC, the consequences of this pain point are starting to show. The scarcity and poor quality of Chinese web content translate directly into scarce, low-quality training data for AI models. And AI training urgently needs high-quality data—otherwise it's just garbage in, garbage out. With compute resources locked down and Chinese content both scarce and low in quality, you can't help but worry about where China will end up in the coming AI era.

#### Buy a Mac—a risky piece of advice

> "Computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes." — Edsger W. Dijkstra

Every tech creator and every comment section online will advise you against buying a Mac—they'll even mock and belittle the idea. You'll hear that a Mac can't run Cinema 4D, can't run industrial software, has no real productivity, that anything a Mac can do Windows can do too while Windows does plenty that a Mac can't—and on top of that, getting a Mac basically means saying goodbye to gaming. Even "[Biba Review Lab](https://space.bilibili.com/175301983)" (笔吧评测室), a famous tech channel with millions of followers, gives this example: if a professor drops some ancient piece of software in DingTalk for a lab assignment, Mac users are left staring helplessly at their screens. And since everyone around you is on Windows, professors aren't going to go out of their way to cater to Mac users or show you how to find alternatives on macOS.

Everything they say is true, and I have to admit it—yet I still recommend that every CS student buy a Mac. It's true that CS coursework in China isn't very Mac-friendly, but the real question should be: why are universities still teaching with ancient 32-bit Windows software? Most of those programs haven't been updated in years, or already have newer, better replacements. MIT has a very famous course called [The Missing Semester of Your CS Education](https://missing.csail.mit.edu/), which specifically teaches students how to use their tools. That's something missing from undergraduate education in China—we never have a class that teaches students to process information using Linux commands, edit text with Vim (controversial, sure, but I still find it genuinely useful), write READMEs in Markdown, or use Git (critically important—something every CS student has to master). As the course website puts it, learning how to use your tools is even more important than learning the material itself. The tools I just mentioned either only exist natively on Unix-like systems like Mac or Linux, or provide a far better experience there. There's a huge difference between being *able* to do something and doing it *well*. Compared to China, universities abroad have a much better ecosystem for Macs: not only do professors use Macs in class, but lab manuals also include setup instructions for students on macOS.

Those incompatibilities certainly caused me plenty of trouble on a Mac. When I was taking Computer Networks, the professor gave us a Huawei packet-tracing lab simulator that didn't exist on macOS. That forced me to go look for an alternative—and since the lectures were based on Huawei's tool, I had to figure out how to use the replacement on my own. Still, after a quick search, you immediately discover a program called [Cisco Packet Tracer](https://www.netacad.com/courses/packet-tracer) that does the exact same thing and has tons of tutorials online. Not only did I find an alternative, I also honed my skills in searching for information online and learning proactively, while getting to know the major players in networking like Huawei and Cisco. Later, when studying Oracle Database, Oracle didn't even have a native release for Mac—and you couldn't just swap it out for MySQL or MongoDB. I spent a huge amount of effort figuring out ways to set up Oracle on a Mac, and even wrote a blog post detailing those workarounds in hopes of helping other Mac students. Along the way, I learned how to SSH into my roommate's computer and use the Oracle installation on his machine—which not only let us collaborate on projects, but meant I didn't have to install anything extra locally. I learned to rent a server on Azure, spin up a virtual machine, set up Oracle inside it, and connect remotely over SSH. Using SSH public and private keys for endpoint authentication deepened my understanding of computer networks. I also discovered amazing tools like Docker and tried running an Oracle image with it—though back then, Docker didn't yet have an Oracle image built for M1 Macs.

##### What does a Mac actually give you?

A Mac gives you a Linux-like environment so you no longer have to pull your hair out setting up a development environment. I still remember the agony in sophomore year of configuring C in VS Code on Windows—it took me an entire week, and even after I finally got it working, random bugs kept popping up. For a student just stepping into computer science, that kind of friction is pure torture. A Mac also gives you a mouse-free experience, a thin-and-light chassis, and battery life that lasts all day. What that means in practice is that you can take it anywhere on a whim—no need to pack a mouse and mousepad, no need to lug around a power brick, and it runs silently without disturbing anyone. One thing I actually think our university did really well was cutting power to the dorms during the day to encourage students to get out of their rooms. Getting out of the dorm and studying in a study hall, the library, or a café boosts your productivity to a completely different level—the dorm simply isn't a good place to study. But for students using Windows laptops, especially gaming laptops, taking a computer out of the dorm becomes a miserable chore. Once you start thinking about packing the charger, mouse, and mousepad (okay, maybe you can skip the pad), and worrying about whether there will be a power outlet where you're going, you quickly lose the motivation to bring your laptop at all. And for CS students, there's not much you can do without a computer, so after weighing all that hassle, you end up staying in the dorm telling yourself you can study there just fine. As it happens, the library and classrooms at our school didn't even have power outlets (well, strictly speaking they did, usually just two at the front and back of the room—obviously nowhere near enough).

#### What did college actually give me—or what did it offer me?

> "Education is what remains after one has forgotten what one has learned in school." — Albert Einstein

I often resent university education in China, especially undergraduate education. Over these four years of college, I never felt that the university gave me meaningful guidance; instead, it threw up all kinds of obstacles and sent me down wrong paths. That created a terrifying gap compared to the college life I had dreamed of in high school, and I often wondered whether those three years of grinding in high school were even worth it. When graduating with his bachelor's degree from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, the founder of a major Chinese AI company wrote in a letter of advice to younger students that "[undergraduate education in China is not on the verge of collapse—it has already collapsed](https://survivesjtu.gitbook.io/survivesjtumanual/li-zhi-pian/huan-ying-lai-dao-shang-hai-jiao-tong-da-xue)." While that's somewhat exaggerated, it does reflect many of the real issues in China's undergraduate education—problems that aren't unique to colleges, but run through our entire education system. On Bilibili, [Professor Song Hao's Calculus course](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1Eb411u7Fw) has racked up 120 million views to date, and students everywhere joke that "Song Hao saved my Calculus grade." (That said, I have to give credit where it's due: Professor Wu Haiyan, who taught my Calculus class, was in my view the best teacher I had in college—in fact, the entire math department, including the professors who taught my Linear Algebra and Discrete Mathematics courses, taught wonderfully. By contrast, every single professor who taught my computer science courses let me down.)

The most common excuse for poor undergraduate teaching is that universities aren't just teaching institutions—they're also responsible for research. Professors aren't evaluated on how well they teach, but on how many papers they publish and what their impact factors are. That's all professors care about, so most treat teaching as a mere chore—which is why so many of them just read word-for-word off textbook slides from over a decade ago. Of course, this isn't only a Chinese problem; universities all over the world suffer from the same issue, with funding heavily skewed away from teaching staff toward research faculty. Once you enter college, it's genuinely rare to meet a professor who feels any passion for teaching. It often makes me feel deeply grateful for my elementary, middle, and high school teachers—they truly loved teaching and genuinely wanted their students to build a better future. At first, I accepted the research-versus-teaching excuse too, until I recently saw a math PhD from the University of Toronto put it this way: *"Research and teaching are not in conflict. Teaching students is mutually beneficial—it doesn't just pass on knowledge, it sharpens your own grasp of how that knowledge fits together. The biggest beneficiary of teaching is always the teacher. Many of the mathematicians whose names you see in theorems, like Euler, were also renowned educators who trained outstanding students."*

Not only are instructors unwilling to take teaching seriously, the school's lab and practical courses are thrown together just as carelessly. They pay no attention to where students actually are in the syllabus—the lectures and labs don't even line up—nor do they consider students' actual skill levels. No matter what CS class it is, they demand a full-stack system: in Oracle, you have to build a library management system with both a frontend and a backend; in Web Dev, a student management system with both a frontend and a backend; same for Java, C#, MySQL, practicums, and lab courses. Calling the handouts "lab guides" is a joke—they offer zero guidance. Most labs just hand you a list of requirements. Students get the lab prompt, have no clue how to begin, and end up copying something off the internet. When we're studying databases, we should be focusing on database concepts; when we're studying frontend, we should be designing the UI and calling pre-built backend APIs instead of worrying about writing backend CRUD boilerplate. Not only does that actually train students properly, it's also how real-world engineering works. Of course, I'm not saying trying to build a full-stack system is bad—quite the opposite, I think building one independently from scratch teaches you a ton and is totally worthwhile. But that belongs in a dedicated capstone or design practicum with plenty of time allotted to it. Professors seem oblivious to how much time a solo full-stack project takes: the bottleneck isn't difficulty, it's sheer volume. It requires massive time investment—often a full month of work to do it right. By contrast, the labs in [Computer Networking: a Top-Down Approach](http://gaia.cs.umass.edu/kurose_ross/), which I studied on my own, were extraordinarily thorough. The lab manual walked you step-by-step through how to write the code, and every function was already covered in the book. When implementing the transport layer, I didn't have to build the entire five-layer stack myself—I just had to focus on writing the transport-layer logic. The application layer above and the network layer below were already implemented; all I had to do was take the application-layer PDU, perform the transport layer's job, encapsulate it into the corresponding SDU, and hand it down to the network layer. Once I filled in the missing pieces, the whole system ran seamlessly. On top of that, the starter code came with a complete README, helpful debug output, and answers to nearly every issue you might run into. Put simply, if you genuinely studied the chapter, you could finish the lab—and only after completing it do you truly appreciate the elegance of the five-layer network architecture.

#### Another problem in undergraduate education is the lack of quality textbooks in China

> "I learned very early the difference between knowing the name of something and knowing something." — Richard Feynman

One textbook people frequently criticize is Fudan University's linear algebra textbook, which starts right off with determinants—leaving students finishing the entire course with no idea what the subject is actually for or why they're learning it. All they know how to do is compute determinants and matrices, and that's all the exams test either. (My own university used an in-house linear algebra textbook that was even worse—it cut out huge chunks of material and was basically just an exam-prep booklet.) I felt this acutely myself. Linear algebra's importance goes without saying: our world is continuous, and many problems in computer science and engineering are fundamentally linear algebra problems. The most famous linear algebra textbook abroad is [*Introduction to Linear Algebra*](https://math.mit.edu/~gs/linearalgebra/) by Gilbert Strang, who also teaches [the accompanying MIT course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/) with infectious energy and passion—it's widely hailed as a classic. Then there's CMU's flagship course [CS15-213](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~213/), which teaches the foundations of computer systems—covering assembly language, architecture, operating systems, compiling and linking, concurrency, networking, and more—combining both depth and breadth as an introductory systems course. That course also gave rise to the classic textbook [*Introduction to Computer System*](http://csapp.cs.cmu.edu/) (translated in China as *深入理解计算机系统*). But that book didn't appear out of thin air—it grew out of the instructors continuously refining their lecture notes over years of teaching before eventually publishing it. So China's shortage of great textbooks and its poor undergraduate teaching are fundamentally the same problem. Great textbooks are forged through the act of teaching; they don't happen overnight. Precisely because professors aren't willing to take teaching seriously, great textbooks never get written—and the lack of great textbooks in turn leaves other instructors without solid teaching materials to guide their classes.

#### I often wished for pure, uninterrupted time just to study

> "To do something well you have to be able to concentrate on it, and to concentrate on it you need uninterrupted blocks of time." — Paul Graham

I'm not demanding that every single class or campus activity be deeply meaningful—if something isn't useful, it should at least be personally fulfilling or enjoyable. Activities that bring nothing but misery are completely pointless and have zero value. I have no idea what the point of mandatory morning exercises is, what the point of standing at attention during military training is, or what the point of sitting through mandatory lectures is. They don't make me happy, and they don't teach me anything. People say hardship builds character, but not every hardship is worth enduring. Maybe the only value of those activities isn't for the students at all—it's for the school.

Rampant formalism at school didn't start in college—it ran through my entire education—but it was definitely at its worst in college, since elementary through high school at least stayed focused on actual studying. My WeChat Moments turned into a dumping ground for "Youth Study" screenshots. If those so-called study sessions actually taught anything meaningful, I'd gladly acknowledge their value. College students really do still have so much to learn, and there are plenty of topics universities don't cover that students genuinely ought to know about. Those modules could easily have filled that gap, and I'm sure students—at least I—would actually have been willing to watch them. Otherwise, it wouldn't have degenerated into "Youth Screenshot."

This semester, the department offered a course called *Introduction to Computer Science*. At first, seeing it on the schedule made me genuinely happy—I thought the school had finally realized students needed an introductory overview of the whole field of computing, even if it really should have been offered in freshman or sophomore year. Then I walked into class full of excitement, only to hear the instructor rambling about love, careers, and the like. And for *that*, we even had to write a report—after which students were considered to have completed *Introduction to Computer Science*. Honestly, I was furious.

There are just way too many examples like this—all kinds of meaningless reports that aren't only useless, nobody even tells you how to write them. They just hand you a report template; all they care about is having a report on file, and nobody cares what's actually written inside. Writing this now makes me genuinely angry—how did our education end up like this?

I still wish for that ideal ivory tower of a university—I'm deeply dissatisfied with my undergraduate experience. I chose to take the graduate school entrance exam, hoping that graduate education might bring some kind of change. An ordinary person deserves a chance at a quality education too—or at least a decent environment in which to teach myself.
